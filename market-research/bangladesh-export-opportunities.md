# Bangladesh's Best Export Opportunities (Beyond RMG)
*Research date: July 2026*

## Why this matters right now

Ready-made garments (knitwear + woven) still make up **~82% of Bangladesh's exports**, and the country faces a hard deadline: **formal LDC graduation in November 2026**, with the EU's Everything But Arms (EBA) duty-free access only guaranteed through a **three-year transition to November 2029**. After that, standard GSP tariffs (~9.6% on most garments) kick in unless Bangladesh secures GSP+ (which requires the labour/governance/environmental commitments it's currently working through — it has already ratified ILO Conventions 155, 187, and 190 as a first step). WTO estimates put **up to $8B/year (~14% of total exports) at risk** once preferences lapse. That's the backdrop for why diversification isn't optional — it's a live deadline, not a long-term nice-to-have.

Ranked below by a mix of current growth rate, documented market size, and how exposed each sector already is to that 2026–2029 tariff cliff.

## Ranked opportunities

### 1. Leather footwear & finished leather goods — strongest momentum, but bottlenecked at the tannery
- Leather footwear exports up **28.96% YoY** (Jul 2024–May 2025) to $620M; overall footwear sector hit **$1.5B in 2024 at 45% YoY growth**; Bangladesh is now the 18th-largest footwear exporter globally, with government/industry targeting **$5B within 5 years**.
- Global buyers are actively shifting sourcing away from China into Bangladesh — this is a live "China+1" beneficiary, not a speculative bet. **Apex Footwear** alone makes **~15% of the country's footwear exports** (3M+ pairs/year), supplying brand owners and retailers across North America, EMEA, and APAC — it started as a tannery in 1976 and only moved into footwear manufacturing in 1990, which is itself the template for where the value is (finished product, not raw hide).

**Why the sector is "stuck at $1B" despite the demand pull:** the whole leather *complex* (tanning + finished goods) has hovered near $1B in exports for over a decade, while industry experts estimate **$5B is reachable by 2030** — meaning roughly 80% of the sector's realistic potential is currently unrealized. The bottleneck is specific and well-documented, not vague "compliance issues":
- **Leather Working Group (LWG) certification** is mandatory for EU/US market access, and only **~8 Bangladeshi tanneries hold it**, out of roughly 200 operating.
- The reason is the **Savar Tannery Estate's Central Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP)**: of the 1,710 total points in an LWG audit, 300 relate directly to effluent/waste management — the exact area Savar fails. During peak season the estate needs to treat ~40,000 cubic feet of effluent/day against a CETP capacity of only ~25,000 cubic feet — a structural, not incidental, shortfall. A second CETP is being planned but a new facility takes 2–3 years to build.
- The direct financial consequence: uncertified tanneries can't sell finished leather into premium markets, so **most sell raw/semi-processed "wet-blue" leather to China at a 20–30% discount**, where it gets finished and re-exported to Europe and the US at full value — Bangladesh is handing its own highest-margin production step to a competitor.

**Where this leaves the actual opportunity:** footwear *assembly* (Apex-style) can partially route around the tannery bottleneck today by sourcing better-compliant leather inputs and focusing on finished footwear/bags/wallets rather than tanning — which is exactly why footwear is growing 30–45%/yr while the tanning side stays flat. The bigger, less-contested prize is upstream: **whoever gets LWG certification first captures disproportionate share**, since only 8 competitors currently qualify against enormous unmet demand. Proposed fixes on the table (tax rebates/soft loans tied to certification, fast-tracked CETP upgrade, SME design/branding support to move into finished goods) are the specific levers to watch — this is a sector where the constraint is a fixable industrial-policy problem, not a demand problem.

### 2. Pharmaceuticals — generics + API, with a closing window
- Export earnings reached **$213M in FY25** (+3.74% YoY, more than double FY18's $103M), now reaching **166 countries**, backed by a growing base of US FDA / UK MHRA / EU GMP-certified plants.
- Bangladesh's TRIPS waiver (letting it manufacture patented generics royalty-free as an LDC) also lapses around the 2026 graduation — so there's a real time-boxed advantage to lock in generic-drug export contracts and API (active pharmaceutical ingredient) capacity now, before both the tariff and IP protections shift.
- Sweet spot: generics for African and South/Southeast Asian markets that don't require the most stringent regulatory dossiers, plus API import-substitution manufacturing rather than finished-formulation-only exports.

### 3. Frozen shrimp & processed seafood — riding a real demand spike
- Seafood export value rose **19.3% YoY to $388.7M**, explicitly driven by **stronger demand from India and China** (23,238 MT of frozen shrimp, +21.5% by volume) — this is happening right now, not a forecast.
- Caveat: raw shrimp production itself is structurally declining (projected ~16,618 MT in FY26-27, versus 54,891 MT in FY10-11) due to farming/environmental constraints — so the opportunity isn't "export more raw shrimp," it's moving up the value chain into **value-added, ready-to-cook/processed frozen seafood** for the India/China corridor, where the processed-food segment overall is growing ~16.6%/year even as raw commodity volumes shrink.

### 4. Jute diversified products (JDP) — a genuine sustainability-trend beneficiary
- Bangladesh is the **world's #1 exporter of jute and jute products** (and #2 producer). Jute bag exports are growing **~20%/year**, and the global jute bag market alone is projected at **$3.2B by 2026** with eco-friendly jute products broadly seeing a ~21.3% CAGR.
- This directly overlaps with the plastic-bag-replacement trend already showing up in India's consumer market (see the earlier dropshipping research — jute/eco bags are a named India trend). Bangladesh is positioned to supply that demand at the industrial/wholesale level, not just retail.
- The specific product lane is **JDP** (~300 distinct product lines: home textiles, geo-textiles, fashion accessories, packaging) rather than raw jute fiber/yarn, where margins are thin. The government-backed **Sonali Bag** (a 100%-biodegradable jute-based bioplastic, with a ৳100-crore investment targeting 1M bags/day capacity) is the highest-profile example of where this is headed — a genuine plastic-replacement export product, not just a "green" bag.

### 5. Light engineering — bicycle parts and machine components
- The light engineering sector grew at a **22–28% CAGR (2017–2022)**, reaching **$795.6M in exports by 2022**. Bicycles are the single largest item in that sector: exports went from $45.2M (2004) to **$192.6M (2022)** — a ~4x increase in under two decades.
- Government policy explicitly targets a 40%-higher GDP contribution from this sector by 2027, making it one of the more actively state-supported diversification bets.
- Opportunity is specifically **bicycle components/parts and light machinery parts** as a China+1 supply-chain play, not finished consumer bicycles competing head-on with established brands.

### 6. IT/ITES and software export services — the real gap is monetization, not capability
- Bangladesh's domestic ICT *market* is ~$9.44B (growing 6.25% CAGR) with **4,500+ IT/ITES firms employing 750,000+ people**, and it's now the **world's second-largest supplier of online freelance labour** (estimates range 650,000 to over 1 million active freelancers, exporting to 80+ countries).
- The statistics on actual export earnings disagree sharply depending on source, and *that gap is itself the story*: Bangladesh Bank's officially recorded computer-services exports were **$724.6M in FY25** (badly missing the government's own $5B target), while industry/payment-processor estimates put the real sector at **$1.4–1.6B and growing ~40%/yr**. The difference between the official and the real number is money that's actually being earned but never gets counted as formal export revenue.

**Why the gap exists — this is a solvable payments problem, not a talent problem:**
- **PayPal does not support withdrawals to Bangladeshi banks at all**, due to Bangladesh Bank foreign-exchange regulations — freelancers there can't even legally hold a PayPal account. Everyone routes through Payoneer, Wise, or direct bank SWIFT instead.
- Those channels aren't free: freelancers lose an estimated **2.8–6.3% of every international payment** to bank fees, exchange-rate markups, and correspondent charges.
- The result: **an estimated 30–40% of freelance income moves through informal channels** — hundi networks, crypto conversions, or foreign-held accounts — specifically to dodge that leakage. None of that shows up in official export statistics, understating the sector and denying Bangladesh forex-reserve credit for work it's already doing.
- Partial fixes already in motion: the **Payoneer–bKash partnership** lets freelancers withdraw straight to a mobile wallet; **Eastern Bank** offers the tightest FX spread with same-day BDT crediting for Payoneer transfers; **BRAC Bank** is the preferred channel for Upwork/Fiverr contractors needing multi-currency SWIFT without monthly fees. The government also pays a **4% cash incentive** on formally-declared freelance/software export earnings through 55 approved platforms — but only up to $5,000 per transaction, which caps its usefulness for larger contracts.

**The infrastructure paradox:** Bangladesh already built dedicated capacity for this — and it's sitting empty. The flagship **Bangabandhu Hi-Tech City** (355 acres in Kaliakair, built for 100,000 workers) currently sees only ~5,000 workers/day doing "low-level" work like data entry and assembly, with buildings visibly decaying. Regional parks are worse: the entire 162-acre Sylhet site is vacant, only 1 of 10 buildings is rented in Rajshahi, and Chattogram Software Technology Park has ~120,000 sq ft sitting empty. Causes cited: missing surrounding infrastructure, bureaucratic red tape, and low investor awareness of the incentives on offer. There's a genuine inflection point though — **since the August 2024 political shift, the Kaliakair park alone has drawn over $800M in investment from 50 firms**, so this may be turning around.

**Proof the talent ceiling is much higher than "cheap freelance labor":** several Bangladesh-based teams are already delivering enterprise-grade, vertical-specialized work that disproves the "low-value BPO" stereotype — **Therap** runs the entire engineering org (product, QA, DevOps, data) for a US electronic-health-record platform serving 300,000+ care providers out of Dhaka; **Selise** (Swiss HQ) runs its flagship global engineering center from Dhaka for banking/insurance/telecom clients; **BJIT** does embedded systems work including VLSI and CAD design; **Vivasoft** delivers cloud-native/Kubernetes enterprise architecture for North American and European clients; **Kaz Software** specializes in healthcare IT, fintech, and EdTech for US clients. None of these are gig-marketplace commodity work.

**Where the real opportunity sits:** not in growing the freelancer headcount (already large, already competitive against India/Philippines/Pakistan on price) but in (1) **fixing the payment rails** — expanding direct-to-wallet channels like Payoneer–bKash and pushing for regulatory movement on PayPal access, so the $1.4B+ that's already being earned actually gets counted and credited as formal export revenue — and (2) **moving firms up-market** from general freelance gig work into the vertical BPO/ITES niches where Bangladesh already has proven anchors (healthcare IT/EHR, embedded/VLSI, regulated fintech, enterprise digital transformation), selling "compliant enterprise delivery for regulated industries" rather than "cheap coding hours." The underused hi-tech park capacity is exactly the physical infrastructure that up-market push would need — if the red-tape and awareness problems get fixed, which the post-2024 investment jump suggests may already be starting.

### 7. Ceramics & home textiles — real but smaller, thinner data
- Ceramic tiles and sanitary ware are established export lines but represent a much smaller segment than the above, with limited hard 2026 figures publicly available — worth tracking but not sizeable enough yet to lead a diversification strategy.
- Home textiles (bedsheets, towels, terry products) already sit at ~3.3% of the garment-adjacent export basket — a natural adjacency to existing RMG expertise/supply chains rather than a new sector, lower risk but also lower upside than the six above.

## The strategic read

- **Most exposed to the LDC deadline**: RMG and leather (both EBA-dependent) — diversifying *within* leather toward finished goods is a hedge against the same tariff risk it's trying to escape, so it only works if paired with quality/design upgrading, not volume growth alone.
- **Best "growth is already happening, just ride it" picks**: leather footwear and frozen seafood (India/China demand) — both have real, current double-digit YoY growth already in motion.
- **Best "underexploited capacity" pick**: IT/ITES — the gap between a $9B+ domestic tech sector and <$1B in actual exports is the widest capability-vs-monetization mismatch in this list.
- **Best "aligned with a global consumer trend" pick**: jute diversified products — it's the one sector where Bangladesh's traditional strength (jute) directly intersects a live global sustainability/plastic-replacement trend rather than just competing on cost.
- **Time-boxed opportunity**: pharmaceuticals — the TRIPS waiver and LDC-linked advantages make the next 2–3 years a use-it-or-lose-it window for locking in generic-drug and API export relationships before the regulatory environment shifts.

Sources: World Bank, WTO, The Business Standard, The Daily Star, East Asia Forum, LightCastle Partners, SeafoodSource, Mordor Intelligence, and sector-specific trade/industry reports (see search citations in session).
